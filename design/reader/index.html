Field Notes·Chapter 01

Reading a pattern

A short practice in looking carefully, describing what is there, and keeping our first explanation provisional.

Illustrative reading · 4 minutes Local reader specimen

01 / Starting point

Begin with a question

A pattern is easy to recognize after someone has pointed it out. The harder work is noticing it before a name has narrowed our attention. This small chapter offers a repeatable pause: look, describe, compare, then decide what to ask next.

Imagine three marks on a page: one close to the margin, one near the center, and one close to the opposite edge. We do not yet know whether they belong together. We only know where each mark appears.

Pause & ask

What did you observe directly, and what did you add as an explanation?

02 / First pass

Notice before naming

A useful description stays close to the evidence. “The marks are farther apart from left to right” records a visible relation. “The marks are accelerating” proposes a story about how they came to be there. The story may be right, but it needs more support.

Try writing one sentence for what is visible and a separate sentence for what might explain it. Keeping those sentences apart makes revision feel like learning rather than failure.

Keep in view

A careful description can be useful even when the cause is still unknown.

03 / Two accounts

Compare the accounts

Two descriptions can fit the same small set of marks. Put them side by side, then ask what each one would lead you to check.

Two ways to describe the same marks
View Description Useful next question
Observation The gaps grow from one mark to the next. Would the spacing stay similar in another sample?
Interpretation The sequence may follow a repeated rule. What other marks would that rule predict?

On a narrow screen, scroll the comparison sideways to read every column.

05 / A short exercise

Try it yourself

  1. Choose a small pattern in an everyday scene, such as the spacing of objects on a shelf.
  2. Write down two details you can point to without explaining them.
  3. Offer one possible account, then write a question that could help distinguish it from another account.

The exercise is complete when you have a better next question. Certainty is not required.

06 / Closing note

Reflection

Good reading leaves room for a second look. We can make a clear statement about what is visible, stay modest about what it means, and still move forward with purpose.

Carry this distinction into the next chapter: description gives an account of the evidence; interpretation offers a reason for it. Both matter, and they do different work.
